First identify and isolate the primary outfit worn by the person in the reference image. Ignore and completely remove the person's face, hair, skin, hands, legs, body, pose, accessories unrelated to the clothing, and the original background.

Carefully analyze the clothing and reconstruct the complete garment as a standalone fashion piece. Preserve the recognizable identity of the original outfit as accurately as possible, including its overall silhouette, proportions, neckline, collar, sleeves, cuffs, shoulder shape, front opening, buttons, pockets, waistline, major panels, layering, hemline, patterns, and other distinctive construction details. Infer naturally any portions of the garment that are hidden by the person's body or pose.

Transform the reconstructed clothing into a handmade FLAT washi-tape and decorative-paper fashion collage.

The garment must look physically assembled from real pieces of washi tape, Japanese paper, thin decorative paper, translucent paper, and textured craft paper, rather than like fabric with a digital paper texture applied to it.

### SIMPLIFY THE GARMENT INTO LARGE PAPER SHAPES

Visually simplify and abstract the original garment into a SMALL NUMBER OF RELATIVELY LARGE, CLEAR PAPER SHAPES.

Do not reconstruct every seam, fold, wrinkle, or tiny construction detail individually.

Instead, analyze the garment as a graphic composition of major shapes.

Whenever possible, represent one visually continuous area of the garment with one larger piece of paper.

For example:

- the main bodice can be one large paper shape;
- each sleeve can be one separate paper shape;
- a collar can be one or two simple paper shapes;
- a waistband can be one horizontal strip;
- a large skirt can be represented by one large silhouette or several broad overlapping panels;
- trousers can be represented by a waist section and two large leg shapes;
- a jacket can be simplified into a body shape, sleeves, collar, and a few major front panels.

Use the FEWEST paper pieces necessary while still preserving the recognizable design and construction of the original garment.

Prioritize the overall silhouette, proportions, color blocking, major structural divisions, and distinctive design features over small details.

The result should feel visually reduced, graphic, calm, and intentional.

Avoid mosaics made from many tiny fragments.

Avoid excessive segmentation.

Avoid constructing the garment from dozens of narrow paper strips.

### ONE MATERIAL PER MAJOR SHAPE

Each major paper shape should preferably be cut from ONE continuous piece of paper or washi material.

Choose the paper surface according to the visual and material character of that part of the original garment.

Translate textile qualities into corresponding paper qualities.

For example:

soft matte cotton → soft matte washi paper;

smooth solid-colored fabric → clean solid-color Japanese paper;

plaid fabric → subtle printed plaid washi paper;

striped fabric → striped decorative paper;

floral fabric → restrained botanical or floral printed washi;

denim → slightly fibrous blue-gray paper;

wool or tweed → softly textured fiber paper;

silk or satin → thin smooth paper with a very subtle soft sheen;

lace → delicate patterned translucent paper;

chiffon, organza, or sheer fabric → translucent Japanese tissue paper;

heavy structured fabric → slightly denser matte craft paper.

Do not reproduce fabric photorealistically.

Instead, reinterpret the visual character of each textile through the texture, transparency, print, fibers, and surface qualities of real paper.

### PRESERVE THE ORIGINAL CLOTHING

Preserve the original garment's overall color identity and important color relationships.

Translate the source colors into slightly softened, natural paper colors while remaining recognizably faithful to the reference image.

Do not arbitrarily redesign the outfit.

Do not introduce unrelated colors, patterns, decorations, pleats, bows, ruffles, pockets, or layers that are absent from the original garment.

Preserve important plaid, stripe, floral, graphic, woven, or repeating patterns when they contribute significantly to the garment's identity, but simplify them into believable printed or textured paper surfaces.

Small functional details such as buttons, narrow trims, ties, pocket openings, or closures may be represented with small simple paper shapes when they are visually important.

### COMPLETELY FLAT PAPER CONSTRUCTION

Keep the entire collage FLAT.

All paper pieces should remain visually flat and exist on the same two-dimensional plane.

Do not create three-dimensional paper sculpture.

Do not fold paper to create volume.

Do not create raised pleats.

Do not curl paper corners.

Do not create floating edges.

Do not create sculptural collars or sleeves.

Do not use accordion folds.

Do not create dimensional ruffles.

Do not attempt to simulate the physical volume of the human body.

Pleats, gathers, folds, draping, and wrinkles in the original garment should be SIMPLIFIED GRAPHICALLY rather than physically constructed.

For example, a pleated skirt should preferably become one large skirt shape or a few broad paper panels, with subtle printed lines, tonal changes, overlapping flat shapes, or changes in paper pattern suggesting the original pleat rhythm.

The result should remain visibly handmade, but its handmade character should come from the PAPER ITSELF rather than from three-dimensional construction.

Use subtle natural paper fibers, slightly imperfect hand-cut contours, tiny irregularities along edges, faint printed textures, translucent areas, and gentle variations in paper surface.

Paper edges should remain visually flat.

### COLLAGE LANGUAGE

The garment should read immediately as a handmade paper collage rather than a realistic wearable garment.

Keep the shapes simple and clearly legible.

Favor broad silhouettes and large uninterrupted paper surfaces.

Allow subtle overlaps between major shapes where the garment genuinely contains layers, but keep those overlaps flat and restrained.

The collage should have a delicate handmade quality without becoming messy, fragmented, distressed, or chaotic.

Avoid excessive torn-paper effects.

Most shapes should appear carefully hand-cut with subtly imperfect edges.

The aesthetic should sit between fashion collage, Japanese paper craft, material study, and minimal graphic abstraction.

### COMPOSITION AND TRANSPARENT BACKGROUND

Present the complete reconstructed outfit alone, without a human body or mannequin.

Isolate the garment completely from its original environment.

Place the garment vertically in the center of the canvas.

The garment should occupy approximately 45–55% of the image height, leaving generous transparent empty space on every side.

The entire area outside the garment must be TRUE TRANSPARENCY with a clean alpha channel.

There must be NO physical background surface behind the garment.

No paper background.

No white background.

No ivory background.

No colored background.

No studio backdrop.

No floor.

No wall.

No table.

No sketchbook page.

No canvas texture surrounding the garment.

Do not simulate transparency using white, gray, beige, or a checkerboard pattern.

The empty area surrounding the garment must contain fully transparent pixels.

Preserve the tactile paper fibers, printed patterns, subtle color variation, and handmade surface texture INSIDE the garment itself.

The transparency requirement applies only to the area outside the reconstructed garment.

Any intentionally translucent washi-paper sections within the garment may retain appropriate partial transparency where this reflects the material character of the original clothing.

Keep the outer silhouette clean and clearly separated from the transparent background while preserving slightly imperfect hand-cut contours.

Do not add a cast shadow beneath or around the garment.

Do not add a halo, glow, outline, border, or artificial edge around the garment.

Do not add additional objects around the garment.

The final result should function as a clean isolated transparent PNG asset that can be placed directly over another photograph, illustration, poster, or background.

### LIGHTING AND SURFACE APPEARANCE

Use soft, even, neutral illumination only to reveal the subtle material qualities within the paper pieces.

Avoid directional lighting.

Avoid dramatic highlights.

Avoid dimensional shading.

Avoid cast shadows.

The paper surfaces themselves may contain subtle natural fibers, slight pigment variations, faint printed textures, and delicate handmade irregularities.

These material characteristics must remain contained within the garment silhouette and must not generate a visible background.
